Scheduling

The scheduler decides which Ready task runs. Its policy determines how quickly an urgent task gets the CPU, which is the property every real-time guarantee rests on.

Learning objectives
  • List the scheduling points at which the kernel makes a decision.
  • Define fixed-priority preemptive scheduling and cooperative scheduling, and compare their latency and their effect on shared data.
  • Explain time slicing (round-robin) among tasks of equal priority.
  • Explain the role of the system tick and of tickless operation.
  • Read a scheduler timeline and identify preemptions, context switches and deadline misses.

Scheduling points

The scheduler does not run continuously. It runs at scheduling points, which are the only moments when the running task can change:

  1. The running task blocks (waits on an object or delays) or terminates.
  2. A task becomes Ready: another task or an ISR gave a semaphore, sent a message, set event bits, or a timeout expired.
  3. The running task yields explicitly.
  4. The tick interrupt fires and the time slice of the running task has expired.
  5. A task's priority changes (explicitly, or by priority inheritance).

When the decision happens inside an ISR, the actual switch is postponed until the outermost ISR returns. The ISR itself is never interrupted by a task.

Fixed-priority preemptive scheduling

Fixed-priority preemptive scheduling: each task has a priority assigned by the designer. At every scheduling point the scheduler runs the highest-priority Ready task. If a task with a higher priority than the running task becomes Ready, it preempts the running task immediately; the preempted task returns to Ready.

This is the default policy of almost every RTOS. Its key property: the response time of a task depends only on tasks of higher or equal priority (and on ISRs and on blocking, covered in Part III). Adding a low-priority task can never delay a high-priority one. That is what makes timing analysable.

Cooperative scheduling

Cooperative (non-preemptive) scheduling: the running task keeps the CPU until it blocks, terminates or yields. A higher-priority task that becomes Ready waits for that moment. Priorities only decide who runs next.

Many kernels support both, per task: a few cooperative tasks for short, non-interruptible sequences, and preemptive tasks for everything else.

Time slicing

Time slicing (round-robin scheduling): among Ready tasks of the same priority, each runs for at most one time slice (quantum); when it expires, the running task moves to the back of the queue for its priority and the next equal-priority task runs.

Time slicing never lets a lower-priority task run while a higher-priority one is Ready. It only shares the CPU fairly between equals. Without it, an equal-priority task waits until the running one blocks. In real-time designs, equal priorities are usually avoided for tasks with deadlines, so time slicing matters mostly for background work.

The system tick

Delays, timeouts and time slices are measured by the kernel's clock. Classically a hardware timer interrupts at a fixed rate (the tick, for example every 1 ms), and the tick handler checks for expired timeouts. A tickless kernel instead programs the timer for the next deadline that actually matters, which saves power because the CPU is not woken for ticks where nothing happens. Either way, a delay of n ticks guarantees at least the requested time, rounded up to the tick resolution.

Try these experiments:

  1. Preemptive, defaults. High is released at t = 3 and runs at once, preempting Medium. Every High job completes within 2 ticks of release.
  2. Switch to Cooperative. Low starts at t = 0 and keeps the CPU until its 9 ticks of work are done. High, released at t = 3, waits until t = 12 and misses its deadline at t = 13. The ISR at t = 7 still runs on time (interrupts preempt even cooperative tasks), but the deferred handler it wakes must wait too.
  3. Preemptive, second low task, time slice 2. The two Low tasks alternate every 2 ticks when nothing more important is Ready. High and Medium are unaffected.

High's response time (release to completion) is 2 ticks, which equals its execution time: nothing outranks it except the handler and ISR. Medium's response time for its first job is 6 ticks: 4 of its own work plus 2 ticks of High's. Low absorbs all the interference and finishes last.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting priority to mean "more CPU time". Priority means "runs first when Ready". A high-priority task that blocks most of the time uses very little CPU.
  • Giving every task the same priority and relying on time slicing. Response times then depend on every other task, which destroys the analysability preemption gives you.
  • Long cooperative sections. In a cooperative task, every loop that does not block or yield adds to the latency of every other task.
  • Assuming a delay is exact. A delay ends at the next tick boundary after the requested time, and the task then only becomes Ready: it runs when it is the highest-priority Ready task.
  • Forgetting the ISR load. Interrupts outrank all tasks; heavy ISR work reduces the CPU available to every task, including the most urgent one.

What this becomes in Zephyr

Zephyr on the STM32F769I-DISCO
  • Priorities: lower number = higher priority. Negative priorities are cooperative (K_PRIO_COOP(n)), zero and positive are preemptible (K_PRIO_PREEMPT(n)). The ranges come from CONFIG_NUM_COOP_PRIORITIES (default 16) and CONFIG_NUM_PREEMPT_PRIORITIES (default 15).
  • A cooperative thread can still be interrupted by ISRs, and it gives up the CPU when it blocks or calls k_yield(). A preemptible thread can make a section temporarily non-preemptible with k_sched_lock() / k_sched_unlock().
  • Time slicing: CONFIG_TIMESLICING=y, CONFIG_TIMESLICE_SIZE in milliseconds, and CONFIG_TIMESLICE_PRIORITY (threads with a higher priority than this are not sliced). It can also be changed at run time with k_sched_time_slice_set().
  • Tick rate: CONFIG_SYS_CLOCK_TICKS_PER_SEC. On this board the Cortex-M SysTick drives the kernel clock and the kernel is tickless by default.
